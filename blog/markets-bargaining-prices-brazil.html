Markets, Bargaining & Prices in Brazil: What Things Really Cost

Quick answer

Street fairs and markets are a big part of Brazilian life — look for a city’s Mercado Central, hippie markets (feira hippie) and, in Salvador, the Mercado Modelo by the Elevador Lacerda. Bargaining is expected at markets and with street and beach vendors, but not in regular shops. Handicrafts and food are much cheaper than in the U.S., while designer and high-quality goods cost about the same as in Europe.

  • Mercado Modelo (Salvador): free entry, roughly 9am–6pm Mon–Sat, shorter on Sunday
  • Bargain at markets and with street/beach vendors — not in shops or malls
  • Food and crafts: cheap. Designer goods: European prices
  • Pay with Pix or cash; carry small bills
  • Ask before photographing stalls or vendors

Street fairs and markets

Street fairs are woven into Brazilian life. In most cities, search for a Mercado Central (central market) for food, cheeses, sweets and cachaça, and a feira hippie (hippie fair) for crafts, clothes and street food — they’re often on weekends and draw big local crowds. Big city markets are a feast of cheeses, doce de leite, cachaça and sweets.

Mercado Modelo, Salvador

Salvador’s best-known craft market sits in a former customs house on the waterfront in the Lower City, right next to the Elevador Lacerda and the Terminal Náutico (where boats leave for Morro de São Paulo). Inside are more than 250 stalls selling hammocks, lace, carved wood, berimbaus, capoeira gear, Bonfim ribbons, spices and cachaça.

  • Entry: free.
  • Hours: roughly 9am–6pm Monday to Saturday and 9am to early afternoon on Sundays (hours vary slightly by source — check on the day).
  • Tip: haggling is expected. Watch your belongings — the area around the market and the Elevador is a known pickpocketing spot.

How bargaining works

Bargaining is a big part of shopping at markets and with anyone selling something who isn’t an official store — especially on the beach. A good approach:

  1. Ask the price: “Quanto custa?”
  2. Smile and offer around 20–30% less on crafts and souvenirs.
  3. Meet in the middle. Buying several items earns a better price.
  4. Walk away politely if it doesn’t work — often the price follows you.

In shops, malls and supermarkets prices are fixed. For street food, simply confirm the price before it’s made.

What to buy

  • Hammocks (redes) — colorful, light and genuinely useful.
  • Bonfim ribbons (fitas do Bonfim) — a few reais for a bundle at stalls by the Bonfim church or the Mercado Modelo.
  • Handmade bags, cushions and textiles in bold tropical prints.
  • Ceramics and carved wood from local artisans.
  • Cachaça, doce de leite and local sweets — pack bottles carefully.
  • Beachwear and Havaianas — Brazilian brands are often better value here.

What things cost

Prices in Brazil range from very cheap to expensive. Higher-quality and designer items are priced about the same as in Europe — imported goods can cost more than at home. Handicrafts and food are much cheaper, especially compared to the U.S. As a rough guide in 2026:

  • Street snack or acarajé: about R$15–25 (~US$2.5–4)
  • Coconut water on the beach: a few reais to around R$10 (~US$1.5)
  • Local beer at a bar: around R$10–15 (~US$1.5–2.5)
  • Lunch at a self-service “por quilo” restaurant: paid by weight, typically R$40–80 (~US$7–13)
  • Museum entry in Salvador: around R$10–20 (~US$1.5–3.5)

Paying: cash, cards and Pix

Cards work almost everywhere in cities, and Brazil runs on Pix, an instant bank-transfer system used by everyone from shops to street vendors. Visitors without a Brazilian bank account can’t always use Pix, so carry some cash in small bills for markets, beach vendors and islands. Use ATMs inside banks, malls or supermarkets during the day.

A note on how streets look

If you’re used to very polished neighborhoods, Brazilian streets may surprise you at first. There are upscale areas too, but overall streets and houses look a bit worn — and also warm, colorful and full of soul, with plenty of local places for every taste. It’s part of Brazil’s unique, artistic beauty.

Frequently asked questions

Can you bargain in Brazil?

Yes, at markets and with street and beach vendors. Prices in shops, malls and supermarkets are fixed.

Is the Mercado Modelo in Salvador free?

Yes, entry is free. It is open roughly 9am to 6pm Monday to Saturday, with shorter hours on Sunday. Haggling is expected.

Is Brazil expensive compared to the U.S.?

Food and handicrafts are much cheaper than in the U.S., while designer and high-quality goods cost roughly the same as in Europe, and imported items can cost more.

Do I need cash in Brazil?

Cards are widely accepted in cities, but carry some cash in small bills for markets, beach vendors and islands, where small sellers may be cash-only.
