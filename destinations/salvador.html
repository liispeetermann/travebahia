Salvador is the soul of Bahia. Founded in 1549 as Brazil’s first capital, it is the most African city in the Americas — you hear it in the drums, taste it in acarajé fried in palm oil, and see it in the baroque churches and candy-colored mansions of the Pelourinho, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1985. The city sits on a cliff above the Bay of All Saints, split into an Upper City and a Lower City, and most U.S. visitors start their Bahia trip here.

Why visit Salvador

Few cities pack this much into one walk. In a single afternoon you can wander the cobbled squares of the Pelourinho, ride the 1873 Elevador Lacerda down to the waterfront, browse the Mercado Modelo and catch the boat to an island. Evenings belong to live samba-reggae, sunset at Farol da Barra and moqueca in Rio Vermelho. In Bahia there are drummers on the streets every day, and the performances are amazing — follow the sound.

The Pelourinho and historic center

The Upper City’s historic heart is a maze of pastel façades, baroque churches and squares — the Terreiro de Jesus, Largo do Pelourinho and Largo do Cruzeiro de São Francisco. Allow at least half a day.

  • Catedral Basílica (Terreiro de Jesus): Jesuit-built in the 1600s, 13 altars; around R$10 (~US$1.5).
  • Igreja do Rosário dos Pretos: the blue church built by Black brotherhoods; small contribution of about R$5 (~US$0.84).
  • Casa do Carnaval da Bahia: a lively museum of Salvador’s Carnival; around R$20 (~US$3.5), free on Wednesdays.
  • Fundação Casa de Jorge Amado: the beloved Bahian novelist’s foundation, on the Largo do Pelourinho.
  • Igreja e Convento de São Francisco: famous for its gold-leaf interior, but closed since a ceiling collapse in February 2025, with reopening expected around 2029 — admire the façade.
  • Balé Folclórico da Bahia: an outstanding Afro-Brazilian dance show in a small theater (around R$100 (~US$17), evenings several days a week — book ahead).

Elevador Lacerda and the Lower City

The art deco Elevador Lacerda (72 m) links the Upper City’s Praça Tomé de Souza with the Lower City in about 30 seconds. It reopened in February 2025 after renovation and now costs R$1 (~US$0.17) per ride. The upper platform is a free viewpoint — come before sunset. At the bottom are the Mercado Modelo craft market (free, about 9am–6pm Mon–Sat, shorter Sundays) and the Terminal Náutico for boats to the islands. Watch your belongings in this busy area.

Bonfim, Ribeira and the bay

  • Igreja do Senhor do Bonfim: Salvador’s most beloved church (free). Tie a colorful fita to the railings, make three wishes, and wear one on your wrist until it falls off. The Lavagem do Bonfim procession takes place on the second Thursday of January.
  • Ribeira: a laid-back waterfront neighborhood famous for its ice cream.
  • Solar do Unhão / MAM-BA: a colonial estate turned modern art museum, free, with one of the best sunsets over the bay.
  • Dique do Tororó: a lake with giant sculptures of Orixás rising from the water (free).

Barra, the lighthouse and the beaches

The Farol da Barra, at the tip of the peninsula, is one of the oldest lighthouses in the Americas; its fort houses the Museu Náutico (R$20 (~US$3.5)). At sunset, locals gather on the walls and applaud as the sun drops into the bay. Next door, Porto da Barra is the city’s calmest, most sheltered beach. Further along the coast are Ondina, Rio Vermelho, Itapuã and Flamengo.

Rio Vermelho: food and nightlife

Salvador’s bohemian neighborhood is where locals go out. Queue for acarajé at the stands of the Largo de Santana (around R$15–25 (~US$2.5–4)), eat moqueca, and bar-hop on the lively streets. On February 2, the Festa de Iemanjá fills the beach with offerings to the sea goddess. See our Bahia food guide.

Day trips from Salvador

  • Ilha de Itaparica: passenger launch from the Terminal Náutico (about 40 minutes) or the car ferry from São Joaquim (about 1 hour).
  • Bay schooner tours to Ilha dos Frades and Itaparica: around R$100–180 (~US$17–30) per person.

Where to stay in Salvador

Barra is the easiest base for first-timers: walkable seafront, beaches, restaurants and quick rides to the historic center. Ondina is quieter, with big seafront hotels. Santo Antônio Além do Carmo and the Pelourinho put you inside the postcard — wonderful by day, best navigated by app ride at night. Rio Vermelho suits food lovers and night owls. Getting around

From the airport (SSA), an app ride or taxi to Barra takes about 25–40 minutes and costs roughly R$70–120 (~US$12–20). In the city, Uber and 99 are the easiest and safest way to get around; the metro links the airport area with the center but doesn’t reach Barra or the Pelourinho. Wear good shoes — the historic center is steep and cobbled.

Staying safe in Salvador

Salvador is a big city with real petty-crime issues, but millions of visitors enjoy it every year with a few habits: keep your phone out of sight on the street, leave jewelry at home, use Uber or 99 after dark instead of walking between neighborhoods, stay on busy, lit streets in the Pelourinho at night, and always ask the price before accepting ribbons or photos. Where to stay

Where to Stay in Salvador: The Best Neighborhoods for Visitors

First-timers should stay in Barra: a walkable, well-lit seafront with beaches, the lighthouse, restaurants and easy app rides everywhere (about 15–20 minutes to the historic center). Ondina next door is quieter, with big seafront hotels. Choose Santo Antônio Além do Carmo or the Pelourinho for the city’s most beautiful boutique hotels — restored mansions with rooftop pools and bay views, and the highest-rated stays in Salvador on TripAdvisor — and Rio Vermelho for food and nightlife. Expect around R$350–800 (~US$59–135) a night for good mid-range hotels and R$700–3,500 (~US$120–590) for high-end.

Salvador FAQ

How many days do you need in Salvador?

Three to five days covers the Pelourinho, Barra, Rio Vermelho, the Bonfim church and a day trip on the bay. Add more if you want Praia do Forte or cultural nights like the Balé Folclórico.

Is the São Francisco church in Salvador open?

No. The Igreja e Convento de São Francisco has been closed since a ceiling collapse in February 2025, with reopening expected around 2029. You can still see its façade.

How much does the Elevador Lacerda cost?

R$1 (~US$0.17) per ride for visitors since its 2025 reopening. The upper platform is a free viewpoint.

Do you need to speak Portuguese in Salvador?

Basic Portuguese helps a lot; English is limited outside upscale hotels. Download Google Translate’s offline Portuguese pack and learn a few phrases like “obrigado/obrigada” (thank you) and “quanto custa?” (how much?).
